Compressed Sofa Landed Cost Breakdown for Importers

Published: 2026-07-15 · Category: Landed Cost & Freight Planning

How buyers compare FOB, carton cost, CBM, freight, import charges and reserve planning before approving a compressed sofa order.

Why landed cost matters more than FOB alone

Many compressed sofa quotations look comparable until buyers add freight, import charges, warehouse handling and return-risk reserve. A lower factory price does not always create the better landed result, especially when carton volume, package count or local delivery limits are different.

For importers, distributors and online furniture brands, landed-cost planning is where pricing, packaging and logistics start to connect. This is also where a sourcing team can catch weak assumptions before the order reaches production.

Buyer rule: Compare landed cost at the SKU level, not only the FOB line on the quotation. The carton profile can change the real result.

1. Build the full landed-cost stack before approval

A practical landed-cost sheet for compressed sofas usually includes the factory FOB price, packaging-related cost, ocean freight, import duty or tax, customs and destination charges, local delivery handling, and a reserve for damage or returns. The goal is not to predict every cent perfectly. The goal is to compare suppliers and models with the same logic.

2. CBM and carton data often decide the freight result

When buyers compare two similar compressed sofa models, the carton data is often where the real difference appears. Carton size, package count, gross weight and CBM per unit affect ocean freight, warehouse handling and local delivery constraints.

The safest approach is to request packing data together with the quotation, not later. That avoids rework after sample approval or carton artwork changes.

Importers planning full-container shipments should also compare 40HQ references with caution. A container-loading estimate is useful, but it is still a planning reference until the final SKU mix and packing layout are confirmed.

3. Where landed-cost comparisons usually go wrong

The most common mistake is comparing FOB prices while ignoring carton volume. Another frequent problem is using old packing data after the sample, fabric or retail packaging file has changed. Buyers also underestimate the operational effect of relabeling, claims, or final-mile handling when cartons are larger than expected.

FOB-only comparison

A quote that looks cheaper at the factory can become more expensive after CBM and local handling are added.

Outdated packing assumptions

Sample revisions, barcode requirements or carton redesign can change both cost and loading efficiency.

No reserve for claims

New programs should include a reasonable allowance for damage handling, relabeling or return exposure.

4. What buyers should request before approving a quote

Ask the supplier for the carton size, package count, gross weight, CBM per unit, 40HQ loading reference, label scope and any cost effect from OEM packaging. If the order is still in inquiry stage, send the information in your first RFQ so suppliers reply with comparable data.

Commercial data

FOB price, MOQ, sample rule and lead-time logic should match the intended order structure.

Packing data

Carton size, CBM, gross weight and package count should be attached to the quotation file.

OEM scope

Confirm barcodes, carton marks, logos and artwork revisions before you freeze the landed-cost sheet.

FAQ

What is included in compressed sofa landed cost?

Landed cost usually includes factory price, packaging cost, freight, import duty or tax, customs-related charges, local handling and an allowance for damage or return-related risk.

Why is CBM important when comparing sofa quotations?

CBM affects ocean freight and warehouse planning. A lower FOB price can still produce a higher landed cost if the carton volume is larger.

When should buyers recalculate landed cost?

Buyers should recalculate after sample approval, carton changes, SKU mix changes, freight updates or any revision to packaging, labels or destination handling assumptions.
